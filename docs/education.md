<section class="ihp-section" id="outreach-plan">
    <div class="section-title"><h2>Education Outreach Plan</h2></div>
    <p class="section-description">We planned two programs, matched to students&rsquo; ages and prior knowledge: a creative DNA game for younger children and a synthetic biology workshop for secondary school students.</p>

    <div class="impact-text">
        <h3>1. Lower grades: kindergarten and elementary school</h3>

        <div class="problem-table-wrap">
            <table class="problem-table">
                <tbody>
                    <tr><td>Schools</td><td>New Taipei Shuangxi Elementary School and Nantou Yuchi Elementary School (kindergarten and elementary students)</td></tr>
                    <tr><td>Format</td><td>Art creation</td></tr>
                    <tr><td>Props</td><td>Boxes labeled &ldquo;eye color&rdquo;, &ldquo;fur color&rdquo;, &ldquo;ears&rdquo;, &ldquo;wings or no wings&rdquo; and &ldquo;special trait&rdquo;, each holding illustrated cards</td></tr>
                </tbody>
            </table>
        </div>

        <h4>Purpose</h4>
        <p>To introduce young children to the basic idea that every living thing has DNA, which contains information that helps make us who we are. Through a simple, interactive game, students explore how living things can share similarities while still having their own unique characteristics. The activity aims to build curiosity about biology and help children understand that differences between individuals are a natural and important part of life.</p>

        <h4>Game</h4>
        <p>Students are first introduced to DNA through a simple, age-appropriate demonstration of the double helix. Each student then randomly selects several illustrated &ldquo;DNA cards&rdquo;. Each DNA-strand-like card represents an instruction for a characteristic, such as body color, ears, eyes, wings, or a tail. Students connect their cards and use the characteristics they show to draw and create their own unique creature. At the end, students compare their creatures and discuss how receiving different combinations of instructions resulted in different characteristics.</p>

        <h3>2. Higher grades: junior and senior high school</h3>

        <div class="problem-table-wrap">
            <table class="problem-table">
                <tbody>
                    <tr><td>Schools</td><td>VIS Experimental Education Junior High School Section and Taipei Municipal Guting Junior High School (junior high); VIS Experimental Education Senior High School Section (senior high)</td></tr>
                    <tr><td>Format</td><td>Workshop</td></tr>
                </tbody>
            </table>
        </div>

        <h4>Purpose</h4>
        <p>The high school biology curriculum generally focuses on entry-level cell biology, genetics, evolution, and ecology. Students in the same classroom may therefore start with very different levels of prior knowledge, depending on their previous schools, courses, and learning experiences. Synthetic biology and its practical applications are also not typically covered in depth. This program aims to give students an accessible introduction to synthetic biology that accommodates these different starting points, so that they can build a common foundation and explore how biological concepts can be applied to real-world problems.</p>

        <p>The program aims to help students understand the basic process of DNA cloning, from DNA amplification through restriction enzyme digestion, ligation, transformation, and selection. It also shows how genetic components can be assembled into a desired biological system, and how such systems are applied across industries: producing recombinant proteins and pharmaceuticals, developing engineered microorganisms, improving agricultural products, and creating biological tools for research and environmental applications.</p>

        <h4>Workshop flow</h4>
        <ol>

            <li>Why do we clone DNA?</li>
            <li>The process of DNA cloning
                <ul>
                    <li>Isolation of DNA</li>
                    <li>Cutting DNA</li>
                </ul>
            </li>
        </ol>

<section class="ihp-section" id="lower-grades">
    <div class="section-title"><h2>Kindergarten &amp; Elementary School</h2></div>
    <article class="hp-community-event">
        <div class="hp-event-copy">
            <p class="hp-event-kicker">NEW TAIPEI SHUANGXI ELEMENTARY SCHOOL &middot; NANTOU YUCHI ELEMENTARY SCHOOL</p>
            <h3>What makes every living thing unique?</h3>
            <p>Our lower-grade program focused on introducing students to DNA, the molecule that carries genetic information and helps shape the characteristics of living things. To start, students brainstormed what makes each individual unique, using a crime scene from a detective cartoon as the scenario. We then explained the science behind phenotypes, using our own appearance and the genes we inherit from our parents. Together, we discussed traits we have inherited from our parents and how differences in the heritability of traits affect how we look.</p>
            <p>Students then put what they had learned into practice through a creative creature-making activity. Each student randomly selected four illustrated DNA cards representing different traits, such as eye color, body color, ear shape, and whether their creature had wings or a tail. By combining the traits on their cards, students created and drew their own unique monsters. Once their creations were complete, they compared their creatures with one another and saw how different combinations of instructions led to different characteristics. Through this activity, students learned about DNA and biological variation while expressing their creativity and discovering the diversity of living things.</p>
            <p>Later in the activity, students predicted the offspring of two distinct monsters by comparing the heritability of their traits. New monsters were created, and the effects of inherited traits were clearly displayed. At this stage, some students noticed that their original &ldquo;parent monster&rsquo;s&rdquo; traits were almost completely hidden. We used this to explain dominant and recessive genes, and related it to how some of their own facial features look more like their mother&rsquo;s while others look more like their father&rsquo;s.</p>
        </div>
    </article>
</section>

<section class="ihp-section" id="higher-grades">
    <div class="section-title"><h2>Junior &amp; Senior High School</h2></div>
    <article class="hp-community-event">
        <div class="hp-event-copy">
            <p class="hp-event-kicker">VIS EXPERIMENTAL EDUCATION JH &amp; SH SECTIONS &middot; TAIPEI MUNICIPAL GUTING JUNIOR HIGH SCHOOL</p>
            <h3>From DNA to real-world solutions</h3>
            <p>What if we could use biology to develop new medicines, improve agriculture, or help solve environmental problems? Synthetic biology explores these possibilities by combining biology, engineering, and design to create or modify biological systems for specific purposes. Our higher-grade outreach program introduced students to synthetic biology and showed them how scientists use DNA to develop new tools and technologies.</p>
            <p>Because students had different levels of biology knowledge, the workshop began with the basics, such as the central dogma, before moving on to more advanced concepts and real-world applications. Students explored DNA cloning, a fundamental technique in biological research and synthetic biology: why scientists clone DNA, and how specific DNA sequences are copied, fused, and introduced into host cells. The workshop covered the main stages of cloning (DNA isolation, amplification, restriction enzyme digestion, ligation, transformation, and selection), using visual explanations and guided activities to show how these steps work together to help scientists build and study genetic systems.</p>
            <p>Beyond the laboratory, students were introduced to how synthetic biology is used in different fields, including producing recombinant proteins and pharmaceuticals, developing engineered microorganisms, improving agricultural products, and creating biological tools for scientific research and environmental applications. By connecting the fundamentals of DNA cloning with real-world examples, the workshop aimed to help students see biology as more than a classroom subject: a field where scientific knowledge, creative thinking, and engineering come together to develop new technologies that benefit our lives.</p>
        </div>
    </article>
